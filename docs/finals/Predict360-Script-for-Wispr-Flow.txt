SLIDE 4: How it works
  DIVIJ (2:10–2:45): Here's how it works, left to right. We record only when keys go down and come up. We turn that into three signals: key hold, pauses, and rhythm. We compare them with your own usual. Then a small model, with just four weights and trained on real typing, gives the score. Claude only explains the numbers in plain words. Sahaj, how well does it work?

SLIDE 5: Results
  SAHAJ (2:45–3:30): Here are the results. We tested on 3,060 check-ins from 51 people the model never saw, with every method set to the same 10 percent false alarms. On the left, the share of changes each method catches. Always saying usual catches nothing. A hand-set rule catches 37 percent. Our first model, trained on simulated typists, also 37. Our model, trained on real typing, catches 59 percent. On the right: overall accuracy is 0.83, where 1 is perfect, the rule gets 0.79, and guessing gets 0.5. Our 95 percent confidence interval for that 59 is 57 to 61. And big changes, where key holds are 20 percent longer, are caught 90 percent of the time.

SLIDE 6: Where it fails
  SAHAJ (3:30–4:15): But it still misses 41 percent of changes. Top left: mostly rhythm changes, because rhythm wobbles 26 percent for the same person on a normal day, while key hold wobbles only 4. Top right: our first model scored 88 percent on simulated typists and was no better than the rule on real people. That's why we retrained on real typing. Bottom left: people differ a lot. The rule's false alarms ranged from 10 to 43 percent per person, so we only compare you with yourself. And bottom right: a flag means a change, not tiredness. We injected the changes, because no public typing data has fatigue labels. Divij, what's next?
