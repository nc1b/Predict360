SLIDE 4: How it works
  DIVIJ (2:10–2:45): Here's how it works, left to right. We record only when keys go down and come up. We turn that into three signals: key hold, pauses, and rhythm. We compare them with your own usual. Then a small model, with just four weights and trained on real typing, gives the score. Claude only explains the numbers in plain words. Sahaj, how well does it work?

SLIDE 5: Results
  SAHAJ (2:45–3:30): Here are the results, on 3,060 check-ins from 51 people the model never saw. Every method is set to the same 10 percent false alarms. On the left: always saying usual catches nothing, a hand-set rule catches 37 percent, our first model trained on simulated typists also 37, and ours, trained on real typing, catches 59. So we miss 41 percent of changes, with 10 percent false alarms. On the right: accuracy is 0.83, where 1 is perfect and guessing is 0.5. Our 95 percent confidence interval for the 59 is 57 to 61. And big changes are caught 90 percent of the time.

SLIDE 6: Where it fails
  SAHAJ (3:30–4:15): Where it fails. First, it misses most rhythm changes, because rhythm wobbles 26 percent for the same person on a normal day, while key hold wobbles only 4. Second, simulation misled us: our first model scored 88 percent on simulated typists and was no better than the rule on real people, so we retrained on real typing. Third, fairness: our data has no age or sex labels, so we checked each person instead. False alarms ranged from 10 to 43 percent, so we compare everyone only with themselves. And last, a flag means a change, not tiredness. Divij, what's next?

SLIDE 7: What comes next
